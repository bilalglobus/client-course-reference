## Overview (Description)
**Job Hazard Analysis (JHA)** is 8 hours of self-paced job hazard analysis training online from the American Institute of Safety Professionals (AISP) that teaches a proven technique for finding hazards before they hurt someone. You learn to select and prioritize jobs, break each job into steps, identify what can go wrong in every step, choose controls with the hierarchy of controls and turn the result into a written safe job procedure that crews actually use.

OSHA defines a JHA as a technique that focuses on job tasks as a way to identify hazards before they occur, looking at the relationship between the worker, the task, the tools and the work environment ([OSHA 3071](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3071.pdf)). It matters because hazards hide in routine work: in 2024, 5,070 workers died from work-related injuries in the United States, one every 104 minutes ([BLS Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries, released February 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cfoi.nr0.htm)). A well-run JHA program catches those hazards at the planning stage, when they are cheapest and easiest to fix.

The course supports compliance across general industry and construction. It helps you meet the workplace hazard assessment and written certification required for PPE selection under [OSHA 29 CFR 1910.132(d)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132), provides documented evidence that recognized hazards are being addressed under the [General Duty Clause](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/section5-duties) of the OSH Act, and applies the [NIOSH hierarchy of controls](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/hierarchy-of-controls/about/index.html) so that elimination and engineering fixes come before PPE.

## How do you conduct a job hazard analysis?
OSHA 3071 sets out a simple, repeatable method. The course teaches each step with worked examples, so you can run a JHA on your own jobs the day you finish.
1. **Involve the workers who do the job.** Bring in the people who perform the task. They know the shortcuts, the awkward steps and the near misses, and their involvement builds ownership of the controls. The JHA evaluates the job, not the worker.
2. **Pick and rank the jobs to analyze.** Review injury, illness and near-miss history, fix any immediately dangerous hazards on the spot, then rank jobs by likelihood and severity. Start with jobs that have high injury rates, could cause severe harm, are new or changed, or are complex enough to need written instructions.
3. **Break the job into steps.** Watch the job being done and list each step in order, starting each with an action verb. Keep steps detailed enough to show every action but not so detailed that the list becomes unworkable, then check the list with the workers.
4. **Identify the hazards in each step.** For every step ask: What can go wrong? What are the consequences? How could it arise? What else contributes? How likely is it? Consider energy sources, struck-by and caught-in risks, chemical and noise exposure, ergonomics and the work environment.
5. **Select controls using the hierarchy.** Eliminate or substitute the hazard where you can, then apply engineering controls, then administrative controls, and use PPE as the last line of defense. Review proposed controls with the workers before you adopt them.
6. **Write the safe job procedure and keep it current.** Turn the analysis into a clear safe job procedure, brief the crew and get sign-off. Review the JHA periodically, after any injury, illness or close call, and whenever the job, tools or conditions change, and retrain workers on every revision.

**Sample JHA worksheet: grinding a small steel part on a bench grinder**

| Job step | What can go wrong (hazard) | Controls |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Inspect the grinder and wheel | Cracked or wrong-speed wheel bursts in use | Check wheel speed rating against spindle speed, inspect the wheel for cracks (ring-test any new wheel before mounting), confirm guards are in place; tag out a defective grinder |
| 2. Adjust the work rest and tongue guard | Part or fingers pulled between wheel and rest | Set the work rest no more than 1/8 inch and the tongue guard no more than 1/4 inch from the wheel, with the grinder off |
| 3. Start the grinder | Wheel failure at start-up strikes the operator | Stand to the side of the wheel during start-up; let it reach full speed before use |
| 4. Grind the part | Flying particles; hand contact; hot metal | Safety glasses plus face shield; hold small parts with a clamp or locking pliers; keep the rest adjusted as the wheel wears |
| 5. Shut down and clean up | Contact with a coasting wheel; grinding dust | Wait for the wheel to stop; clean with a brush or vacuum, not by hand |

*Illustrative example. Work rest and tongue guard limits are from OSHA 29 CFR 1910.215. Your JHA must reflect your own equipment, manufacturer instructions and workers' input.*

**The hierarchy of controls:** Elimination: remove the hazard → Substitution: replace it with something safer → Engineering controls: isolate people from the hazard → Administrative controls: change the way people work → PPE: protect the worker with equipment

## FAQ
**What is a job hazard analysis (JHA)?**  
A job hazard analysis is a technique that focuses on job tasks as a way to identify hazards before they occur ([OSHA 3071](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3071.pdf)). You break a job into steps, identify the hazards in each step and decide the controls that eliminate or reduce them. The result is usually written up as a safe job procedure that workers are briefed on.

**Why is a job hazard analysis important?**  
Because most serious injuries happen during routine tasks whose hazards were never written down. In 2024, 5,070 US workers died from work-related injuries, one every 104 minutes ([BLS CFOI](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/cfoi.nr0.htm)), and private employers reported about 2.5 million nonfatal injuries and illnesses ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/2-5-million-workplace-injuries-and-illnesses-in-private-industry-in-2024-down-3-1-percent-from-2023.htm)). A JHA finds those hazards at the planning stage, when they are cheapest to fix.

**Is a job hazard analysis required by OSHA?**  
Not by that name. There is no single OSHA standard that requires a document called a JHA. However, [29 CFR 1910.132(d)](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.132) requires employers to assess the workplace for hazards that need PPE and to certify that assessment in writing, and the [General Duty Clause](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/oshact/section5-duties) requires a workplace free from recognized serious hazards. A JHA is one of the most practical ways to meet both duties and show your work.

**What is the difference between a JHA and a JSA?**  
Very little. In practice the terms are used interchangeably: both break a job into steps, identify hazards and set controls. Some organizations use JSA for a quick pre-task review done in the field and JHA for a more formal, documented analysis. This course teaches the full OSHA 3071 method, which works for either.

**Which jobs should get a JHA first?**  
OSHA recommends starting with jobs that have the highest injury or illness rates, jobs that could cause severe or disabling harm even if no one has been hurt yet, jobs where one simple human error could cause a serious accident, new or changed jobs, and jobs complex enough to need written instructions ([OSHA 3071](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3071.pdf)).

**What questions should you ask to identify hazards in a job step?**  
OSHA 3071 uses five questions for every step: What can go wrong? What are the consequences? How could it arise? What are other contributing factors? How likely is it that the hazard will occur? A good hazard description names the environment, the exposure, the trigger, the consequence and any contributing factors ([OSHA 3071](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3071.pdf)).

**What should a JHA worksheet include?**  
A JHA worksheet should include the job title and location, the people who did the analysis and the date, each job step in order, the hazards in each step, the controls for each hazard, the PPE required, and review and sign-off details. Keep it to one or two pages so crews can actually use it at a pre-task briefing.

**How often should a job hazard analysis be reviewed?**  
Review each JHA periodically even if the job has not changed, and always after an injury or illness, after a close call, and whenever the equipment, materials, process or work environment changes. OSHA also advises training all affected employees whenever a JHA is revised ([OSHA 3071](https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/publications/osha3071.pdf)).

**Who should conduct a job hazard analysis?**  
A supervisor or safety professional who knows the method usually leads the JHA, but the workers who perform the job must be involved at every stage. They understand the task best, they help catch hazards an observer would miss, and they are far more likely to follow controls they helped design.

**What is the hierarchy of controls in a JHA?**  
The hierarchy of controls is the order in which you choose controls, from most to least effective: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls and PPE ([NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/hierarchy-of-controls/about/index.html)). A JHA should always ask whether the hazard can be removed or engineered out before relying on procedures or protective equipment.

**What is the difference between a JHA and a risk assessment?**  
A JHA analyzes one specific job step by step. A risk assessment is broader: it can cover a whole site, process or activity and usually rates each risk by likelihood and severity to set priorities. Many organizations use a risk assessment to decide which jobs need a JHA, then use the JHA to control the hazards in those jobs.

**Does a JHA replace lockout/tagout procedures or permits?**  
No. A JHA identifies hazards and controls, but it does not replace procedures that a specific OSHA standard requires, such as energy control procedures under [29 CFR 1910.147](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.147) or confined space entry permits. A good JHA points to those procedures at the steps where they apply.

**What are the most common job hazard analysis mistakes?**  
The most common mistakes are writing the JHA in an office without watching the job, listing steps that are too broad, describing hazards vaguely ("be careful"), jumping straight to PPE instead of higher controls, leaving out the workers who do the job, and never reviewing the JHA after a change or close call.
